# Ten Advanced Quant Research Papers for ADX Quant Pro

## How to read this document

In this guide, **belief** means the paper's modeling intuition or working assumption. It does not mean that the assumption is always true. **Theorem / mathematical core** distinguishes a formal result from an empirical model. No theorem is invented where the paper is primarily methodological or empirical.

Every proposed addition should begin as a **shadow candidate**. It may read the canonical completed snapshot and write separate research outputs, but it must not change production rank, bias, entry permission, or Field 10 until it passes walk-forward validation, leakage checks, probability calibration, Model Confidence Set testing, Probability of Backtest Overfitting controls, and Deflated Sharpe Ratio review.

## 1. James D. Hamilton — “A New Approach to the Economic Analysis of Nonstationary Time Series and the Business Cycle” (1989)

### Core concept

Hamilton models an observed time series whose dynamics depend on an unobserved discrete regime. A latent Markov chain switches among states such as expansion/contraction or, for this system, `BULL_NORMAL`, `BEAR_NORMAL`, `RANGE_NORMAL`, and `COMPRESSION`. Each state may have different mean, persistence, volatility, and transition probabilities.

### Modeling belief

Financial behavior is not generated by one stable process. It is better represented as several locally stable processes with probabilistic transitions. Regime changes are not known with certainty at the current candle; they are inferred from the full information available up to that candle.

### Mathematical core

Let `S_t` be an unobserved state and `y_t` the observed return or factor vector.

- State transition: `P(S_t=j | S_{t-1}=i) = p_ij`.
- Conditional observation model: `y_t | S_t=j` follows a state-specific distribution or autoregression.
- The Hamilton filter recursively computes filtered probabilities `P(S_t=j | y_1,...,y_t)`.
- A backward smoothing pass may estimate `P(S_t=j | y_1,...,y_T)` for historical evaluation only. Smoothed probabilities must never be used in a live feature for time `t` because that would leak future data.

The likelihood integrates over the latent states and estimates transition and state parameters by maximum likelihood.

### Integration into this system

Create a shadow module that consumes only completed H1/H4 candles and the existing Field 3 feature set. Publish:

- filtered probability for every regime;
- most likely current regime;
- regime age distribution rather than a single guessed age;
- 1H/3H/6H/24H transition probabilities;
- expected remaining duration `1 / (1 - p_jj)` as a model estimate, with uncertainty;
- entropy of the state distribution as regime uncertainty.

Do not replace the existing Field 3 regime. Compare the candidate against it in an audit table. Promote only when transition calibration and out-of-sample decision utility improve.

### Expected benefit

This can replace hard regime flips with probabilities, reduce false transitions, improve the meaning of regime reliability, and make Field 10 risk protection sensitive to transition uncertainty.

### Main risks and validation

The number of states can be overfit; labels can switch; transition matrices can become unstable with short history. Use expanding or rolling walk-forward estimation, state-label alignment rules, minimum occupancy, and Brier/log-loss calibration. Reject the candidate when a simpler regime model is statistically indistinguishable.

## 2. Robert F. Engle — “Dynamic Conditional Correlation: A Simple Class of Multivariate Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity Models” (2002)

### Core concept

Dynamic Conditional Correlation (DCC) separates individual volatility estimation from time-varying correlation estimation. Each instrument first receives a univariate volatility model; standardized residuals then drive a low-parameter dynamic correlation matrix.

### Modeling belief

Cross-symbol dependence is not constant. EURUSD, GBPUSD, AUDUSD, USDCHF, gold, and other selected instruments can become temporarily crowded around USD, risk-on/off, or macro-event factors. A rank that treats six highly correlated positions as six independent opportunities understates portfolio risk.

### Mathematical core

For return vector `r_t`:

- `r_t = H_t^(1/2) z_t`.
- `H_t = D_t R_t D_t`, where `D_t` contains conditional standard deviations and `R_t` is the conditional correlation matrix.
- Standardized residuals are `epsilon_t = D_t^(-1) r_t`.
- A common DCC(1,1) recursion is `Q_t = (1-a-b) Q_bar + a epsilon_{t-1}epsilon_{t-1}' + b Q_{t-1}`.
- Normalize `Q_t` into `R_t` using its diagonal.

Positive-definiteness and parameter restrictions such as `a>=0`, `b>=0`, and `a+b<1` preserve a stable correlation process.

### Integration into this system

Add a cross-symbol DCC shadow service after all selected symbols have passed load validation. It must use synchronized completed candles only. Publish:

- current DCC matrix;
- one-day and one-week change in correlation;
- USD-factor concentration;
- cluster membership;
- effective number of independent bets;
- crowding penalty per symbol;
- portfolio-level risk warning.

Field 10 may display this as read-only evidence. Later, after validation, it can reduce entry permission or position size when top-ranked symbols are nearly the same exposure.

### Expected benefit

It improves multi-symbol reliability, prevents duplicate risk, makes rank diversification explicit, and helps distinguish a genuinely diverse six-symbol set from one concentrated USD trade.

### Main risks and validation

DCC can react slowly, correlations are noisy, and asynchronous market hours can distort estimates. Validate against constant-correlation and exponentially weighted baselines. Use realized portfolio volatility, drawdown, and calibration of correlation forecasts as acceptance metrics.

## 3. Fulvio Corsi — “A Simple Approximate Long-Memory Model of Realized Volatility” (2009)

### Core concept

The Heterogeneous Autoregressive model of Realized Volatility (HAR-RV) represents volatility as an additive cascade across daily, weekly, and monthly horizons. It approximates long-memory behavior with a simple linear structure.

### Modeling belief

Different market participants operate at different horizons. Short-horizon traders respond to daily volatility, swing participants react to weekly conditions, and long-horizon investors respond to monthly conditions. Their combined actions create persistent volatility without requiring a complicated fractional model.

### Mathematical core

A standard specification is:

`RV_{t+1}^{(d)} = beta_0 + beta_d RV_t^{(d)} + beta_w RV_t^{(w)} + beta_m RV_t^{(m)} + error_{t+1}`

where weekly and monthly measures are averages of daily realized volatility. Log-HAR or square-root transformations can improve residual behavior. For H1/H4 use, define compatible aggregates such as 6-hour, one-day, one-week, and 25-day realized measures while preventing overlap leakage in targets.

### Integration into this system

Use genuine intraday OHLC or returns to publish a volatility term structure:

- expected volatility over 1H, 3H, 6H, 12H, 24H, and 36H;
- volatility surprise versus forecast;
- volatility-of-volatility;
- uncertainty-scaled expected-return score;
- data sufficiency and realized-measure quality.

Field 3 can use the term structure as regime evidence. Field 10 can divide directional opportunity by expected volatility and execution cost, rather than ranking raw expected return alone.

### Expected benefit

This should improve risk normalization, expected-return reliability, stop/TP realism, transition-risk estimates, and the interpretation of `INSUFFICIENT LOCAL HISTORY` by specifying the exact horizon whose history is missing.

### Main risks and validation

Realized volatility is sensitive to data quality and microstructure noise. Evaluate QLIKE and MSE in walk-forward tests, compare simple HAR to existing volatility logic, and require stability across symbols and market regimes.

## 4. Jim Gatheral, Thibault Jaisson, and Mathieu Rosenbaum — “Volatility is Rough” (2018)

### Core concept

The paper argues that log-volatility behaves approximately like fractional Brownian motion with a very low Hurst exponent, near 0.1, rather than the smoother processes used in many classical stochastic-volatility models.

### Modeling belief

Volatility is highly irregular at short horizons. Apparent long memory can emerge from a rough, strongly mean-reverting process. Short-horizon risk therefore changes faster and more erratically than a smooth volatility filter implies.

### Mathematical core

The empirical scaling relation considers moments of log-volatility increments:

`E[|log sigma_{t+Delta} - log sigma_t|^q] proportional to Delta^(qH)`

with `H < 0.5`, empirically around 0.1. Rough-volatility models often use a fractional kernel such as `(t-s)^(H-1/2)`. This is not a simple theorem guaranteeing trading profit; it is an empirical structural result and a modeling framework.

### Integration into this system

Add a lightweight roughness estimator rather than a heavy full rough-Heston calibration at first. Publish:

- rolling Hurst/roughness estimate for log realized volatility;
- short-horizon volatility shock score;
- rough-volatility forecast for 1H/3H/6H;
- disagreement against HAR-RV and existing volatility forecasts;
- interval widening factor during rough shocks.

Use the result to widen prediction intervals, reduce trade permission, or lower reliability when short-horizon volatility is much rougher than the production model assumes.

### Expected benefit

It can reduce overconfident Power BI paths, improve short-horizon risk, make uncertainty react faster to news shocks, and improve the protection layer during sudden volatility transitions.

### Main risks and validation

Hurst estimates are unstable in small samples. Do not convert one noisy estimate into a hard regime. Require minimum history, bootstrap uncertainty, and out-of-sample interval-coverage improvement. Compare against simple realized-volatility shock indicators before accepting added complexity.

## 5. Peter R. Hansen, Asger Lunde, and James M. Nason — “The Model Confidence Set” (2011)

### Core concept

The Model Confidence Set (MCS) procedure starts with a set of candidate models and sequentially removes models shown to be inferior until the remaining set contains the best model with a chosen confidence level.

### Modeling belief

Financial samples often do not support a single permanent winner. Several models may be statistically indistinguishable, and choosing the top historical score alone creates unstable model selection.

### Theorem / statistical principle

Given loss differentials among models, MCS tests the null of equal predictive ability. A sequential elimination rule removes the worst model when the null is rejected. Under the procedure's assumptions, the resulting set has asymptotic coverage for the best model at the chosen confidence level. When data are weak, the set remains larger rather than pretending certainty.

### Integration into this system

Apply MCS to shadow candidates by symbol, horizon, and regime using losses appropriate to each output:

- log loss or Brier score for BUY/SELL/regime probabilities;
- pinball loss for quantiles;
- interval score for prediction intervals;
- QLIKE for volatility;
- cost-adjusted utility loss for rank/entry decisions.

Publish `MCS_INCLUDED`, `MCS_EXCLUDED`, p-value, sample window, and competing models. Production should use either the existing model or an ensemble of MCS survivors. Never promote a candidate merely because it ranks first by one backtest mean.

### Expected benefit

This creates a formal promotion gate, reduces model-churn, prevents minor random performance differences from changing production logic, and gives a principled way to retain several valid models.

### Main risks and validation

Loss observations are serially dependent. Use block bootstrap with a justified block length. Avoid tiny regime slices. Require a minimum effective sample and report when MCS is inconclusive.

## 6. Ryan J. Tibshirani, Rina Foygel Barber, Emmanuel J. Candès, and Aaditya Ramdas — “Conformal Prediction Under Covariate Shift” (2019)

### Core concept

Weighted conformal prediction adapts prediction intervals to covariate shift when the test covariate distribution differs from the training distribution but the conditional outcome mechanism is sufficiently stable. Importance weights adjust calibration examples according to their relevance to the current test point.

### Modeling belief

The current market may resemble some historical periods more than others. Uniform calibration treats obsolete and current regimes equally, causing interval undercoverage after distribution shift.

### Theorem / guarantee

Under the covariate-shift setup and known or adequately estimated likelihood ratio between test and training covariate distributions, weighted conformal methods provide finite-sample or near finite-sample marginal coverage according to the method's assumptions. The guarantee is about coverage, not profitability or conditional accuracy for every exact state.

### Integration into this system

Create a weighted conformal layer around existing point predictions:

- nonconformity score: absolute or normalized forecast error;
- covariates: regime probabilities, volatility state, session, spread, event flag, correlation concentration, and trend strength;
- weight estimator: regularized classifier distinguishing recent/current covariates from training covariates;
- outputs: lower/upper interval, target coverage, observed rolling coverage, interval width, effective calibration sample, and shift severity.

Field 2 should display these calibrated intervals as a shadow overlay first. Entry permission should later require both acceptable expected value and calibrated interval geometry.

### Expected benefit

It directly improves reliability of uncertainty bands, adapts to drift, and makes `confidence` empirically testable through realized coverage rather than an arbitrary score.

### Main risks and validation

Extreme weights cause high variance. Clip weights, report effective sample size, and revert to a conservative unweighted interval when overlap is poor. Validate rolling coverage separately by symbol, horizon, and major regime.

## 7. David H. Bailey, Jonathan M. Borwein, Marcos López de Prado, and Qiji Jim Zhu — “The Probability of Backtest Overfitting” (2017)

### Core concept

The paper develops the Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO) and Combinatorially Symmetric Cross-Validation (CSCV). It estimates how often the strategy selected as best in-sample ranks poorly out-of-sample across many symmetric train/test partitions.

### Modeling belief

When many parameter sets, thresholds, models, symbols, or feature combinations are tried, the best backtest is likely to contain selection noise. Ordinary train/test splitting does not reveal how much the selection process itself was overfit.

### Mathematical core

CSCV divides the observations into an even number of blocks, evaluates combinations of half the blocks in-sample and their complements out-of-sample, selects the in-sample winner, then records the winner's relative out-of-sample rank. The distribution of logit-transformed out-of-sample ranks estimates the probability that the chosen model underperforms the median out-of-sample.

### Integration into this system

For every research candidate family, record all tried variants, not just the winner. Run CSCV on:

- Field 10 rank weights;
- probability thresholds;
- regime transition parameters;
- expected-value horizons;
- feature subsets;
- model ensembles.

Publish PBO, number of trials, parameter family, degradation, and rank correlation. Block a candidate when PBO exceeds a predeclared threshold or when the result depends on one favorable split.

### Expected benefit

It protects the system from “improvements” that look excellent only because many alternatives were searched. This is especially important for a large application with many fields and research models.

### Main risks and validation

CSCV assumes a defensible observation/block construction. Use time-respecting blocks, avoid overlapping-label leakage, include transaction costs, and keep an untouched final holdout where possible.

## 8. David H. Bailey and Marcos López de Prado — “The Deflated Sharpe Ratio: Correcting for Selection Bias, Backtest Overfitting, and Non-Normality” (2014)

### Core concept

The Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR) adjusts an observed Sharpe ratio for the number and dependence of trials, sample length, skewness, and kurtosis. It asks whether performance exceeds what could plausibly arise from selection among many noisy backtests.

### Modeling belief

A high ordinary Sharpe ratio is not strong evidence when many strategies were tested or returns are non-normal. The hurdle must rise with research breadth and return-path irregularity.

### Mathematical core

DSR compares the observed Sharpe ratio against an expected maximum Sharpe under multiple trials, then standardizes it using the sampling distribution adjusted for skewness and kurtosis. The result is a probability-like measure that the Sharpe exceeds the selection-biased benchmark. It complements PBO: PBO analyzes rank degradation across partitions; DSR analyzes significance of the selected performance statistic.

### Integration into this system

For each production-promotion candidate, store:

- net returns after spread, slippage, and estimated execution impact;
- number of independent/effective trials;
- observed Sharpe, skewness, kurtosis, and sample length;
- expected maximum Sharpe under selection;
- DSR probability;
- regime and symbol breakdown.

Require a predeclared DSR threshold together with PBO, MCS, and calibration tests. Never use DSR to turn a poor economic idea into a trade merely through statistical filtering.

### Expected benefit

It reduces false research discoveries and provides a consistent evidence standard for changing Field 10 weights, thresholds, or trade permissions.

### Main risks and validation

The effective number of independent trials is difficult to estimate when models are correlated. Use clustering or correlation-adjusted trial counts and disclose sensitivity to this assumption.

## 9. Robert Almgren and Neil Chriss — “Optimal Execution of Portfolio Transactions” (2001)

### Core concept

Almgren–Chriss frames execution as a trade-off between expected transaction cost and the variance of execution cost. A trader selects a liquidation or acquisition trajectory while accounting for temporary impact, permanent impact, price risk, and risk aversion.

### Modeling belief

A correct directional forecast is not enough. Profit can disappear when spread, slippage, market impact, and delay are considered. Position size and urgency should depend on liquidity and risk, not only signal strength.

### Mathematical core

A common linear-impact setup separates:

- permanent impact that shifts the price process with cumulative trading;
- temporary impact that penalizes trading rate;
- price variance accumulated while the position remains unexecuted.

The objective minimizes `Expected Cost + lambda * Variance(Cost)`. The optimal trajectory lies on an efficient frontier between low expected cost and low execution risk. Closed-form schedules arise under linear impact and independent price increments.

### Integration into this system

For retail FX, use a simplified, evidence-based implementation:

- spread and slippage estimate by symbol/session;
- liquidity/volatility proxy;
- expected holding horizon;
- position-size cap;
- urgency score;
- expected execution cost in pips and percentage;
- cost-adjusted expected value;
- `TRADE`, `WAIT`, or `BLOCK` permission.

Do not pretend to know institutional market impact from retail candle data. Treat impact coefficients as conservative calibrated proxies and show their source.

### Expected benefit

It can stop trades with positive raw expected value but negative net expected value, improve position sizing, and make the less-risky decision operational rather than purely directional.

### Main risks and validation

Impact parameters are uncertain and broker-specific. Calibrate from actual fills where available; otherwise use conservative ranges and sensitivity analysis. Never hide cost assumptions.

## 10. Marcos López de Prado — “Building Diversified Portfolios that Outperform Out-of-Sample” (2016)

### Core concept

The paper introduces Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP), which uses hierarchical clustering and recursive allocation instead of inverting a noisy covariance matrix. Instruments are ordered by cluster structure, then risk is distributed recursively.

### Modeling belief

Sample covariance matrices are unstable, especially when the number of assets is not small relative to history. Mean-variance optimization can produce concentrated and fragile weights. Hierarchical structure can produce more robust diversification.

### Mathematical core

HRP generally follows three stages:

1. **Tree clustering:** derive a distance such as `d_ij = sqrt((1-rho_ij)/2)` from correlations and build a hierarchical tree.
2. **Quasi-diagonalization:** reorder instruments so similar exposures are adjacent.
3. **Recursive bisection:** split the ordered set into clusters and allocate inversely to cluster variance until instrument weights are obtained.

The method is algorithmic rather than a theorem guaranteeing outperformance. Its claim is supported through comparative out-of-sample experiments.

### Integration into this system

After selected symbols pass the strict completion contract:

- use DCC or robust correlations to cluster them;
- calculate HRP shadow weights;
- combine HRP risk weight with Field 10 opportunity rank;
- cap currency-factor exposure, symbol weight, and total leverage;
- publish diversification ratio, cluster risk, effective number of bets, and weight stability.

Production promotion should require lower realized drawdown or more stable risk without materially reducing net expected utility.

### Expected benefit

It converts Field 10 from a list of independent ranks into a portfolio-aware decision system. It can prevent six selected symbols from becoming one hidden USD bet.

### Main risks and validation

Clustering can be unstable and distance choices matter. Use bootstrap cluster stability, turnover penalties, and comparisons against equal risk, equal weight, and current sizing.

# Recommended implementation order

## Phase 1 — Reliability gates first

1. Probability of Backtest Overfitting.
2. Deflated Sharpe Ratio.
3. Model Confidence Set.
4. Weighted conformal prediction.

These control whether future model changes are trustworthy.

## Phase 2 — Forecast and regime improvements

5. Hamilton Markov switching.
6. HAR-RV.
7. Rough-volatility shock layer.

These improve regime probability, volatility forecasts, and uncertainty.

## Phase 3 — Multi-symbol portfolio intelligence

8. DCC.
9. Hierarchical Risk Parity.
10. Almgren–Chriss-inspired cost and execution permission.

These convert signal ranking into portfolio-aware, cost-aware decisions.

# Production-promotion contract

A research candidate may influence production only after all checks below pass:

- exact completed-candle identity and no future leakage;
- purged/embargoed walk-forward validation;
- sufficient sample by symbol, horizon, and major regime;
- calibrated probability or interval coverage;
- MCS inclusion at the chosen confidence level;
- acceptable PBO;
- acceptable DSR after realistic costs;
- no material degradation in worst-regime drawdown;
- stable behavior under parameter perturbation;
- shadow run for a predeclared period;
- versioned report and rollback path.

# Recommended Field 10 additions after validation

Do not replace existing columns. Add versioned columns or expanders for:

- filtered regime probabilities and entropy;
- volatility term structure and roughness shock;
- calibrated prediction interval and observed coverage;
- MCS membership and loss rank;
- PBO and DSR research status;
- DCC crowding penalty and effective independent bets;
- HRP shadow weight;
- execution cost, net expected value, and trade permission;
- explicit evidence timestamp, source, run ID, model version, and validation status.
